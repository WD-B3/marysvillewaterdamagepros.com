Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage? A Marysville, OH Guide

Coverage depends on the cause of the water, not just the damage. Here is how to read your policy before you call your insurer.

(740) 231-9132See all services

By the Marysville Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026.

The short answer is that it depends on where the water came from. Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a pipe that bursts or a hose that fails, is often covered by a standard homeowners policy. Gradual leaks and damage from neglect often are not, and flood damage from rising water is usually excluded. The details are in your policy, and this guide explains what to look for.

None of this is legal or insurance advice, and your insurer decides what applies. The goal is to help you ask the right questions and keep the right records, so that a covered loss is not weakened by a gap in the paperwork.

Sudden and accidental versus gradual

Most policies draw a line between a loss that happens suddenly and one that develops over weeks or months. A supply line that splits and floods a kitchen is sudden. A drip under a sink that rots a cabinet base over a season is gradual.

Insurers often treat the first as a covered peril and the second as maintenance, so how you describe the timeline matters. Be accurate, and let the evidence speak.

What flood coverage means

Standard homeowners policies commonly exclude flood, meaning water that rises from outside, such as creek overflow or surface water. Flood is usually covered by a separate policy, such as one under the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood policy.

A wet basement can be a plumbing loss or a flood loss, and the difference affects which policy responds. If the cause is unclear, say so, and let the adjuster investigate.

Questions to ask your insurer

  • Is the cause of my loss a covered peril under my policy?
  • Do I have water backup coverage for sewer or sump failures?
  • What is my deductible for this type of loss?
  • What documentation do you need, and by when?
  • Does my policy pay for emergency mitigation such as extraction and drying?

Keep the records an adjuster looks for

Photographs and video taken before cleanup, a list of damaged items, moisture readings and a log of what was removed and why all help. A restoration crew can provide readings and a written scope, and keeping receipts for emergency steps matters too. Do not throw away damaged materials until you have been told you can, unless they pose a safety risk.

If the water is still spreading, mitigation comes first. Many policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, which includes stopping the source and getting standing water out. See the pages on water extraction and structural drying for what that work involves.

Terms that trip people up

A few words in a policy carry a lot of weight. A peril is a cause of loss the policy names, such as fire, wind or the sudden discharge of water from a plumbing system. An exclusion is a cause the policy says it will not pay for, and flood and earth movement are the usual examples.

An endorsement is an add-on that changes the base policy, which is how water backup coverage is normally added. Reading these three words in your declarations pages tells you more than any summary online.

Limits matter as well. Some policies cap water backup coverage at a low amount, and some set a separate deductible. Contents, the building and living expenses are often listed as separate lines, so a claim can touch several of them. If your home cannot be lived in while it dries, ask whether additional living expense coverage applies and what receipts you need to keep.

What to do while you wait for an answer

Coverage questions can take days, and water does not wait. Take reasonable steps to protect the property: stop the source, remove standing water and start drying. Keep every receipt for emergency work, since many policies reimburse reasonable mitigation costs even before the full claim is decided. Do not start permanent repairs until the insurer has had a chance to see the damage, unless you are told otherwise in writing.

If you disagree with a decision, ask for the reason in writing and the policy language it relies on. You can supply more evidence, such as readings or a plumber's report that shows the cause. For help with a dispute, the Ohio Department of Insurance has a consumer services division that handles complaints.

Key takeaways

  • Sudden and accidental water is often covered, gradual damage often is not.
  • Flood needs its own policy, and sewer backup usually needs an endorsement.
  • Read the declarations page for limits, deductibles and endorsements.
  • Photograph first, mitigate promptly and keep every receipt.
