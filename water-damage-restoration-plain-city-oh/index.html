Plain City by area: the downtown, the parks and the creek

Plain City is a village on Big Darby Creek at the junction of U.S. Route 42 and State Route 161, about 11 miles from Marysville and nearly nine from Dublin. It had 4,065 residents at the 2020 census. The village was laid out in 1818 as Westminster, renamed Pleasant Valley in 1823 and renamed Plain City in 1851 because another Ohio town already used the earlier name.

The older downtown includes the Farmers National Bank building from 1902, which is on the National Register of Historic Places, and the town clock dedicated the same year. Buildings of that age often have older plumbing and basements, so a slow leak can run a long time unseen. Pastime Park and McKitrick Park sit along the village edge, with McKitrick Park directly on Big Darby Creek.

When water is spreading in Plain City

If water is entering a Plain City home or business, call dispatch immediately. Stop the source if you can do it safely, shut off the main water valve and keep people out of wet areas. If the water came from the creek or a storm drain, treat it as contaminated and stay out until it is assessed. Emergency dispatch is available around the clock for the village and the surrounding area.

Two counties, one phone number

Plain City lies in both Madison and Union counties, so permits, inspections and floodplain rules depend on which side of the line a property sits on. If you are planning repairs, confirm the correct office with the village or the county before work starts. Dispatch can tell you whether the kind of work you have in mind is likely to need a permit, and the answer is the same for both sides of the line.

Insurance does not care about the county line. Your policy decides what is covered, and it helps to keep the same set of photos, readings and invoices whichever side you are on.

What the Big Darby Creek corridor means for flooding

Local reporting in 2017 described Big Darby Creek overflowing its banks and flooding McKitrick Park, with the National Weather Service forecasting the creek to rise to nearly ten and a half feet before receding. That is a past event, not a forecast, but it is a reminder that homes near the creek should check the FEMA flood map for the address. Flooding of this kind is Category 3 water, and the flood damage page explains how it is handled.

The village also owns its storm sewers and the outfalls that drain to the creek. In 2025 the village council discussed a possible stormwater utility fee to pay for aging storm sewers and outfalls. An older storm system and heavy rain together are why a basement floor drain backing up is worth treating as an emergency.

Before we arrive: if water is still spreading

Stop the water

Close the shutoff valve at the fixture or the main water valve, and keep clear of any water near outlets.

Protect electricals

Shut off breakers to wet rooms only from a dry spot. If the panel is wet, call the utility or 911.

Lift and move

Raise furniture legs onto blocks and move small items, papers and electronics to a dry floor.

Document the loss

Photograph the water line and the damage before cleanup, then call the 24/7 line.

Housing and buildings in the village

The village mixes older homes and downtown buildings with newer housing along the main roads. Older buildings tend to have original plumbing and basements that hold water. Newer homes more often fail at supply lines and appliances, covered on the burst pipe page and the appliance leak page. Commercial buildings downtown and along U.S. 42 may need the commercial page.

What affects the price in Plain City

The visible conditions decide the scope.

  • Water category. Creek and storm drain water is more contaminated than a broken supply line.
  • Building age. Older buildings have more materials to check.
  • Basement or slab. Lower levels dry more slowly.
  • Business use. Commercial jobs need documentation and scheduling.
  • Rebuild. Repairs follow drying and are priced separately.
