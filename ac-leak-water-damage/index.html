AC Leak Water Damage Repair in Marysville, OH

A clogged condensate line can quietly wet a ceiling, a closet or a hardwood floor for weeks.

(740) 231-9132See all services

An air conditioner leak is rarely dramatic at first. A condensate drain line clogs, the pan fills, and the overflow runs into the ceiling or down a wall for days or weeks before anyone sees a stain. Turn the system off at the thermostat if water is overflowing, then call dispatch at any hour. This page covers the water damage that results, not the repair of the air conditioner itself.

Water from an AC system ends up where the equipment sits: an attic, a closet, a basement utility space or above a finished ceiling. Many calls start as a stain on the ceiling below the unit, which the ceiling water damage page explains in more detail.

How an AC unit leaks

Cooling coils pull moisture out of the air, and that water drains away through a condensate line. If the line clogs with algae and sludge, water backs up into the drain pan and overflows. A rusted pan, a cracked drain fitting and a frozen coil that thaws all cause leaks too. The result is usually clean or slightly stale water, but it often sits in insulation and drywall for a long time.

  • Water stains on the ceiling beneath an attic unit or air handler.
  • A damp or musty smell near a closet that houses the air handler.
  • Water in the drain pan or on the floor around a furnace or air handler.
  • A line of staining down a wall below a wall-mounted or ductless unit.
  • Hardwood flooring near a unit that has started to cup.

When to call right away

If water is dripping from a ceiling, if drywall is soft or sagging, or if electrical equipment is wet, call now. One caller described a condensate line that had been backing up and a hardwood floor that began to cup afterward. Moisture from a slow leak can reach flooring and framing long before it is visible. Turn the system off to stop adding water, and keep away from wet wiring.

What the cleanup involves

The crew locates the wet areas with a moisture meter and thermal camera, since the visible stain is often smaller than the wet area. Wet insulation and drywall that cannot dry in place are removed, and air movers and dehumidifiers go in. Wood floors are dried promptly, and the hardwood floor page explains what is possible.

An HVAC technician clears the line, repairs the pan and checks the equipment. The two trades should coordinate, because drying cannot hold if the leak returns. Ask the technician to confirm that the condensate line is clear before the structure is closed back up.

Preventing another overflow

Condensate lines should be cleared periodically, drain pans inspected and a float switch installed on systems in finished spaces. A technician can recommend a schedule. Attic and closet units are the most likely to cause hidden damage, because no one checks them regularly.

What to ask the HVAC technician

Once the structure is drying, the equipment needs attention too. Ask the technician where the condensate line terminates, whether the pan is rusted or cracked and whether a float switch can shut the system off before it overflows. Ask when the line was last cleared and how often it should be.

Keep the invoice. It shows the date the problem was fixed, which helps if your insurer asks when the leak started and stopped. If an attic unit sits over a finished ceiling, a secondary drain pan with its own drain is worth discussing, because the cost of a pan is small next to a ceiling repair.

What affects the price after an AC leak

The visible conditions decide the scope.

  • Duration. A leak that ran for weeks reaches more insulation and framing.
  • Location. Attic units over finished ceilings add ceiling repair.
  • Flooring. Hardwood near the unit may need drying mats and time.
  • Materials. Insulation and drywall are easier to replace than framing.
  • Drying time. Cavities above ceilings dry slowly.

The scope and price are explained before work starts. Call (740) 231-9132.

Insurance may respond to a sudden overflow and often does not respond to long-term leaks from poor maintenance. Check your policy or ask your insurer, and keep records of any HVAC service.
